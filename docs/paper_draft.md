## 1. Introduction

Cuneiform tablets, among the earliest surviving written records, are frequently damaged, and reconstructing their missing content is a slow, expert-dependent process: roughly half of the half a million cuneiform tablets excavated to date remain untransliterated (Cobanoglu et al. 2024). Where a tablet survives only in fragments, philologists reconstruct the missing signs by hand, drawing on their own recollection of parallel passages and formulaic phrasing — a process that is inherently subjective and does not scale to the volume of unpublished material (Lazar et al. 2021).

This reconstruction task maps directly onto masked language modeling (MLM), the pretraining objective underlying BERT (Devlin et al. 2019): both ask a model to predict a token from its surrounding context. Lazar et al. (2021) were the first to make this connection explicit for Akkadian, framing sign restoration as masked-token prediction rather than designing a bespoke generative architecture for the task.

A parallel line of work in Greek and Latin epigraphy has pursued the same idea with purpose-built architectures. Pythia (Assael et al. 2019) was the first deep-learning system for ancient-text restoration, trained from scratch on Greek inscriptions. Its successor Ithaca (Assael et al. 2022) extended restoration with two attribution tasks — assigning a damaged inscription's geographic origin and its date of writing — within one multi-task architecture. Aeneas (Assael et al. 2025) extended Ithaca further, adding a vision branch that conditions the geographic-attribution head on a photograph of the inscription, and handling gaps of unknown length. For Akkadian, Lazar et al. (2021) established that a fine-tuned multilingual encoder outperforms a from-scratch monolingual one for restoration, but did not extend the task to attribution or to a visual modality. We combine both lines of work in Nabu — named for the Mesopotamian god of writing and patron of scribes — a fine-tuned multilingual BERT, in the spirit of Lazar et al. (2021), extended with Ithaca/Aeneas-style multi-task attribution heads (period, genre, language, provenience) and an Aeneas-style vision-conditioned head, applied to Akkadian for the first time.

Both Ithaca and Aeneas frame their systems as decision-support tools rather than autonomous replacements for philologists, and report that access to model predictions measurably improves human performance: historians given Ithaca's top-20 restoration hypotheses improved their own restoration accuracy from 25% to 72% (Assael et al. 2022). We adopt the same framing — our goal is a tool that narrows the space of plausible readings and attributions for a human expert to evaluate.

## 2. Related Work

Machine learning applied to ancient-language tasks has grown rapidly in recent years, across scripts, tasks, and modalities (Sommerschield et al. 2023); we organize the work most directly relevant to our own system below.

### 2.1 Fine-tuned BERT for Akkadian

Lazar et al. (2021) fine-tuned multilingual BERT (mBERT) on transliterated Akkadian text from the Open Richly Annotated Cuneiform Corpus (ORACC), roughly 10,000 transliterated tablets totalling 1 million words and 2.3 million signs. Predicting masked signs, their fine-tuned model reached 89.5% top-5 accuracy (MRR 0.83, Hit@5 0.89 overall); notably, mBERT's *zero-shot* performance (before any Akkadian finetuning at all) already outperformed a monolingual Akkadian BERT trained from scratch by about 10%, suggesting multilingual pretraining itself, not only the finetuning data, does much of the work. To inject Akkadian-specific vocabulary without retraining mBERT's embedding table, they repurposed 99 of mBERT's reserved but otherwise-unused `[unusedN]` vocabulary slots via the WordPiece algorithm. We adopt the same mechanism for two damage-marker tokens (Section 3) and, going further, use the remaining free slots for 97 Akkadian-specific WordPiece tokens learned directly from our own corpus.

Fetaya et al. (2020) provide direct evidence, on Akkadian administrative and legal texts, that bidirectional context is substantially more informative than left-only context for this task: an LSTM given the full surrounding context reached MRR 0.89, against MRR 0.754 for the same architecture given only the context preceding a gap. This is the strongest available justification, within the Akkadian-restoration literature itself, for a bidirectional encoder such as BERT over an autoregressive, left-to-right decoder.

The general strategy of domain-adapting a pretrained BERT encoder for text attribution by period and region is not unique to Akkadian or to epigraphy: Lendvai et al. (2023) apply the same fine-tuned-BERT-for-attribution approach to unrelated pre-modern Slavic manuscripts, attributing century and region from text alone — direct precedent, outside the epigraphic literature, for the period and provenience classification heads we add in Section 4.

### 2.2 Pythia → Ithaca → Aeneas

Pythia (Assael et al. 2019), a sequence-to-sequence BiLSTM operating on character and word representations jointly, was the first deep-learning system applied to ancient-text restoration; on a held-out set of Greek inscriptions it reached a character error rate (CER) of 30.1%, against 57.3% for expert epigraphists working under the same conditions.

Ithaca (Assael et al. 2022) restructured the task around one shared transformer torso feeding three separate task heads — restoration, geographic attribution, and chronological attribution — trained jointly on the Packard Humanities Institute's ancient Greek inscription corpus. Ithaca alone reaches 61.8% top-1 restoration accuracy (26.3% CER); for geographic attribution, among 84 candidate regions, 70.8% top-1 and 82.1% top-3 accuracy; for chronological attribution, its predictions fall a median of 3 years from the ground-truth date range. This shared-torso, per-task-head design is the direct architectural precedent for the multi-task model described in Section 4.

Aeneas (Assael et al. 2025) replaced Ithaca's torso with a T5-style decoder augmented with rotary positional embeddings, extended restoration to gaps of unknown length, and added a vision branch: a small convolutional network (ResNet-8) processes a photograph of the inscription, and its output is concatenated with the text representation feeding the geographic-attribution head only. Aeneas's authors explicitly exclude the visual input from the restoration head, to avoid the model exploiting unmasked regions of the image to infer masked text, and from the dating head, having found no measurable benefit in ablation experiments. This scoping decision is the direct precedent for the vision-conditioned head described in Section 4; we did not simply adopt it, but independently re-derived the same scoping via our own controlled ablation (Section 5).

A concurrent project, Kyivan (Eremeev and Sychov 2026), applies the same restoration-and-attribution framing to pre-modern Slavic texts (birch-bark letters and several historical charter/subcorpus sources, ~5,400 documents total), predicting dialect and date rather than our own genre/period/provenience/language. Where we fine-tune a pretrained multilingual BERT (Section 4), Kyivan instead trains a from-scratch, character-level BERT encoder augmented with rotary positional embeddings — the same from-scratch strategy we separately built and found unproductive for our own, considerably larger corpus (Section 6).

### 2.3 Vision + cuneiform

Kapon et al. (2024) classify a cuneiform tablet's historical period from its photograph alone — no text — with a ResNet50 model trained on 94,936 CDLI tablet images, reaching 61% macro-F1 against an 8% baseline from a decision tree using only the tablet's height-to-width ratio. This shows that a tablet's physical shape carries a real, learnable period signal through a purely visual channel. Our own results (Section 5) find no corresponding benefit from adding a visual input to a *joint* text+image model's period head; one plausible reconciliation is that text alone, via the language, orthography, and genre cues an mBERT-based model already reads, already captures most of the available period signal, leaving little for a fused image feature to add — unlike Kapon et al.'s vision-only setting, where the image is the model's only available signal.

Dencker et al. (2020) show that individual cuneiform signs can be localized within tablet photographs using only weak supervision — aligning existing transliterations to tablet images, rather than requiring manually annotated bounding boxes — establishing tablet photography at CDLI's own scale as a workable input for computer-vision methods specific to cuneiform.

Verwimp et al. (2025) train and evaluate a ResNet50 classifier for individual Old Babylonian cuneiform sign images, drawn from tablets across three Mesopotamian findspots (Nippur, Dūr-Abiešuḫ, Sippar), reaching 87.1% top-1 and 96.5% top-5 accuracy for signs with at least 20 training instances — the first automated classification result reported for this period. Their central finding, that classification performance degrades substantially when a model trained on one digitization source or scribal hand is applied to another, is a direct caution for any cuneiform vision model — including ours — trained on a corpus assembled from multiple photographic sources rather than one internally consistent collection.

Chen et al. (2024) train both text-based and image-based classifiers for cuneiform period dating on the same CuneiML corpus (Chen et al. 2023) our own vision branch draws on, and identify a failure mode directly relevant to any model trained on tablets pooled across museum collections, including ours: label shift (different collections skew toward different time periods) compounded by covariate shift (different collections' own photography setups leave identifiable artifacts), together driving a large accuracy gap between tablets from collections seen during training (up to 84.42%) and tablets from held-out collections, only partially closed by adversarial regularization and domain adaptation. Williams et al. (2025) build DeepScribe, a two-stage pipeline (RetinaNet localization, ResNet classification) for locating and identifying individual Elamite cuneiform signs in photographs of the Persepolis Fortification Archive, reaching 0.78 localization mAP and 0.80 end-to-end top-5 sign classification accuracy — a sign-level complement to Dencker's and Verwimp's own localization and classification work, applied to a different cuneiform-writing language and period than either. Hagelskjær (2026) approaches cuneiform tablet metadata classification from a different visual modality entirely — 3D point clouds sampled from CAD scans rather than 2D photographs — outperforming a Point-BERT baseline on several classification tasks; 3D scanning captures information a flat photograph cannot (text wrapping around a tablet's edges), but remains far less scalable across the hundreds of thousands of excavated tablets than 2D photography, which is why we, like the large majority of work cited here, work from 2D photographs.

Mahmood and Panok (2025) apply neural machine translation to the reconstruction and translation of damaged Akkadian passages from the Epic of Gilgamesh, reporting that their system performs well on formulaic lines and passages with close parallels elsewhere in the epic, but struggles with rare vocabulary, damaged proper names, and syntactically uncertain lines. On our own curated literary examples (Gilgamesh, Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, the Laws of Hammurabi), top-1 restoration accuracy no longer shows a clear gap against a random sample of the (predominantly administrative) test set after this session's corpus rebuild and retrain — 66%/63% (text-only/vision) on the 8 curated examples against 66%/67% on a 20-example random sample. An earlier version of this comparison, before the rebuild, did show a gap in the direction Mahmood and Panok's finding would predict; with only 8 curated examples the comparison is noisy in either direction, so we report the current numbers as an open observation rather than a confirmed replication.

### 2.4 Restoration for other ancient languages

The transformer-based restoration approach we apply to Akkadian has close analogues for other under-resourced ancient languages and scripts, beyond the Greek/Latin lineage described above. Fono et al. (2024) are, to their own account, the first to apply a masked-language-modeling framing to damaged ancient Hebrew and Aramaic inscriptions, evaluating transformer models fine-tuned on Biblical text at several masking percentages and finding that an ensemble of word-level and character-level prediction heads outperforms either alone — a multi-granularity combination our own single-token-level restoration does not attempt. Gu et al. (2024) fine-tune RoBERTa for restoration of ancient Chinese texts, an architecture choice directly parallel to our own use of a fine-tuned masked-language-model encoder rather than a from-scratch or autoregressive alternative, adding phrase-level semantic features and a KAN prediction layer on top of the base model. Papavassileiou et al. (2023) apply a bidirectional recurrent generative model, benchmarked against n-gram baselines, to infill damaged and incomplete words in Mycenaean Greek texts written in the Linear B script — an Aegean writing system unrelated to cuneiform — verifying their method against ground truth before applying it to genuinely damaged tablets and comparing its output with expert philologists' own judgments, the same historian-comparison framing Ithaca and Aeneas later adopt at much larger scale. Levine et al. (2024) train a bidirectional RNN for character-level restoration of Coptic manuscripts, finding 72% single-character accuracy that falls to 37% for lacunae of varying length, and — rather than treating the model as a stand-alone restoration tool — use it to rank candidate reconstructions already under scholarly consideration, an assistive framing close to our own decision-support goal (Section 1). Hua et al. (2025) combine restoration and chronological attribution in one masked-language-modeling framework for early Chinese bronze inscriptions, adding an allograph-aware component that links distinct glyph forms of the same underlying grapheme — a domain-specific extension with no direct analogue in our own WordPiece vocabulary, but a reminder that vocabulary and tokenization choices matter as much as architecture for restoration quality in these scripts.

### 2.5 Other Akkadian NLP

Several other recent projects apply deep learning to different Akkadian-language tasks: automatic transliteration of Unicode cuneiform glyphs into words, at up to 97% accuracy (Gordin et al. 2020); neural machine translation from Akkadian to English, from both cuneiform glyphs and transliteration directly (Gutherz et al. 2023); and, most recently, bidirectional Akkadian-English translation paired with a tool for rendering composed text as cuneiform (Wang 2026). None of these address restoration or attribution. A 2025 shared task on lemmatization and token prediction for Akkadian and Sumerian (Gordin, Sahala, Spencer & Klein 2025) further confirms that restoration-style prediction is an active benchmarking target for the field.

### 2.6 Data sources

Our corpus draws on two external sources beyond ORACC's own catalogue metadata. CuneiML (Chen et al. 2023) supplies Unicode cuneiform glyph segmentations aligned to CDLI tablet photographs and bounding boxes, and is the basis of the visual input used in Section 4. The electronic Babylonian Library (eBL) publishes a growing, openly licensed (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) transliteration corpus of roughly 25,000 tablets and 350,000 lines (Cobanoglu et al. 2024); we draw on the project's Zenodo snapshot both to recover transliterations for tablets missing usable text elsewhere and for several of our curated literary examples.

## 3. Data

**Evaluation regime.** We evaluate restoration under one regime: 15% of tokens masked at random following BERT's own masking recipe, evaluated on a held-out test split never used during training or checkpoint selection. We do not additionally evaluate against real editorial reconstructions — signs an ORACC editor restored inside square brackets, as distinct from signs plainly attested on the tablet — because our text-processing pipeline does not preserve, in the final corpus, which characters originated from an editor's bracketed conjecture rather than a direct reading; recovering that distinction would require a new parsing pass over ORACC's raw markup, left to future work. Like Aeneas, we do not strip editorial conjectures from the training text either, for the same reason its authors give: excluding them would discard usable data from an already data-scarce domain (Assael et al. 2025).

**Corpus composition.** Our corpus totals 56,934 documents (train 50,959 / validation 2,929 / test 3,046). The majority, 50,698, come from merging and deduplicating ORACC's own editions with CuneiML's; a further 6,236 are drawn directly from CDLI's bulk ATF export and from eBL — recovering tablets whose text existed nowhere else in our sources, balancing underrepresented period/genre/language/provenience classes, and adding a curated set of well-known literary texts (the Epic of Gilgamesh, Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, the Laws of Hammurabi) held out into the test split. 9,245 documents (16.2%) carry a real tablet photograph, reviewed by hand for a correct bounding box; the remainder receive an all-zero placeholder image at training time, following the same design Aeneas uses for the roughly 95% of its own corpus without an image (Assael et al. 2025); every photographed document is presented to the model, but — as detailed in Section 4 — it is used by only one of the four classification heads, provenience.

**Metadata categories.** We map the free-text period, genre, provenience, and language fields recorded by CDLI and ORACC onto four small, fixed category sets by substring rather than exact match: raw catalogue values carry inconsistent suffixes (e.g. "Old Babylonian (ca. 1900-1600 BC)"), and exact matching discarded 30-58% of otherwise usable records to an "Unknown" placeholder. The resulting categories necessarily coarsen CDLI's own finer distinctions to keep each head's class count tractable at our corpus size — our genre category "Literary & Scholarly", for instance, merges CDLI's astrological, omen, ritual, mathematical, and prayer genres. The final sets: nine periods (Neo-Assyrian, Ur III, Old Babylonian, Old Assyrian, Middle Assyrian, Middle Babylonian, Neo-Babylonian, Third Millennium, Late Antiquity); six genres (Administrative, Lexical, Royal Inscriptions, Literary & Scholarly, Legal, Letters); four languages (Akkadian, Sumerian, Bilingual, Peripheral/Other); thirty-six provenience classes, from large well-known sites (Nineveh, Babylon, Ḫattusa, Mari, Ebla, Susa, ...) to smaller but well-attested findspots (full list in Table 2) — a label space comparable in scale to Ithaca's and Aeneas's own geographic-attribution classes (84 and 62 regions respectively). A handful of period and provenience candidate classes considered during development (Middle Hittite, Proto-/Neo-/Middle Elamite for period; nine additional findspots for provenience) were dropped after finding fewer than 50 actual recoverable documents behind their catalogue-scale counts — catalogue counts do not reliably predict actual corpus-scale support once text-recoverability is the real bottleneck, so the reported classes are only those that cleared this bar.

**Missing labels.** A record whose raw value matches none of these categories, or is simply absent, is treated as missing: it is assigned label -100 and excluded from both the loss and the accuracy/F1 computation for that head, rather than defaulted to an arbitrary class. Missing-label rates: 2.0% for period, 13.1% for genre, 19.8% for language, 11.5% for provenience — reflecting how consistently each field is actually populated in the underlying catalogues; language, the least completely populated of the four, is the most affected, with genre a close second.

**Text-level damage markers.** Separately from missing category labels, the transliterated text itself carries two distinct damage signals, which we map to two of mBERT's reserved token slots and exclude from both masking targets and the model's own candidate outputs, since a position marking genuinely unrecoverable content has no ground truth to train against: a single sign of uncertain reading (`x` in the source transliteration, present in 33.4% of documents and 7.55% of word-tokens), and a gap of unknown length (an ellipsis in the source transliteration, present in 21.4% of documents and 2.29% of characters). This mirrors the single-character/unknown-length distinction Aeneas encodes as `-`/`#` (Assael et al. 2025).

## 4. Methods

**[Figure 1: architecture diagram — see "Figures & Tables" section below for the full spec.]**

Our encoder is `bert-base-multilingual-cased`, fine-tuned rather than trained from scratch, for the same reason Lazar et al. (2021) give for the same choice: the available Akkadian corpus is orders of magnitude smaller than what a competitive from-scratch transformer requires. We confirm this empirically for our own corpus (Section 5): masked-token-prediction mean reciprocal rank rises from 0.508, for an otherwise identical but un-finetuned model, to 0.780 after finetuning — fine-tuning does substantial additional work beyond what mBERT's own pretraining alone provides.

Four classification heads — period, genre, language, provenience — are trained jointly with the masked-language-modeling objective on one shared encoder, following the same shared-torso, per-task-head design as Ithaca and Aeneas (Assael et al. 2022; 2025), extended from their two attribution tasks (region, date) to four; genre and language have no direct equivalent in Ithaca/Aeneas's Greek/Latin setting. Task losses are weighted 3.0 for the masked-language-modeling objective and 1.0 for each metadata head — close to, though not copied from, Aeneas's own fixed weighting of 3 for restoration, 2 for region, and 1.25 for date (Assael et al. 2025).

For tablets with a photograph, we add a vision branch: a ResNet18 backbone, initialized from ImageNet and fine-tuned jointly with the rest of the model, whose output is normalized and concatenated with the encoder's `[CLS]` representation feeding the provenience head only. This mirrors Aeneas's design of concatenating a small convolutional network's output into the geographic-attribution head alone (Assael et al. 2025), with two differences. First, we use a larger, ImageNet-pretrained backbone rather than a from-scratch ResNet-8: a from-scratch convolutional network overfit in preliminary experiments given our own photographed-document count (9,245 — comparable in absolute terms to Aeneas's own roughly 8,843, their reported 5% of 176,861 inscriptions — despite our overall document corpus being smaller). Second, we use lighter geometric augmentation — rotation and shear capped at 15°/5°, against Aeneas's reported 30°/10° — after finding the wider range clipped genuine tablet content on our tightly, manually reviewed image crops.

**Similar-document retrieval.** Following Aeneas's own contextualization mechanism, we build a single embedding per document from the trained encoder's own hidden states, with no separate retrieval model. Aeneas defines its embedding as

`emb_context = ( emb_torso[t=1] + (1/N) · Σ_{t=2..N} emb_torso[t] ) / 2`

where `emb_torso[t=1]`, the torso's first output, "aligns with the sentence prefix" and the remaining `t=2..N` outputs align with the textual input (Assael et al. 2025). Our encoder has no such prefix token to repurpose, but `[CLS]` plays exactly the same summary role by construction, so we substitute it directly:

`emb_doc = ( h_[CLS] + (1/(L-1)) · Σ_{i=2..L} h_i ) / 2`

for a sequence of `L` tokens with encoder outputs `h_1 = h_[CLS], h_2, ..., h_L`. Aeneas's authors report that this simple, un-trained embedding outperformed several trained retrieval alternatives (embedding texts and metadata jointly, or as raw inputs) in their own preliminary evaluation, attributing this to their corpus's limited size (Assael et al. 2025); our corpus is smaller still, so we adopt the same design rather than building a dedicated retrieval model. Retrieval itself uses cosine similarity between a query document's embedding and every other document's, over the full corpus (train, validation, and test alike, mirroring Aeneas's own choice to extend retrieval beyond the training set since it does not influence any model prediction).

**Saliency maps.** For explainability, we adopt Aeneas's own choice of gradient-based saliency (the "vanilla gradient" method of Simonyan et al. 2014) over raw attention-weight visualization, which Aeneas's authors note remains disputed as a reliable explanation method in its own right (Assael et al. 2025). For a given prediction — a restored token at a masked position, or a classification head's predicted class — we backpropagate from that single output logit to the token input embeddings and take the L2 norm of the gradient at each position, giving a per-token saliency score with no retraining required. Unlike Aeneas, which reports one saliency map per task (restoration, geographic attribution, chronological attribution), we produce one per head, plus one per restored position, so a reader can inspect exactly which context tokens drove any single prediction. For the vision-conditioned provenience head specifically, we additionally compute a Grad-CAM map (Selvaraju et al. 2017) over the ResNet18 backbone's last convolutional block — the direct convolutional analogue of Aeneas's own image saliency map for its geographic-attribution head — highlighting which region of the photograph most influenced the predicted provenience.

**Restoration error metric (CER).** Aeneas and Ithaca report character error rate for restoration, stratified by masked-span length so that whichever length is most common in their designed-damage protocol cannot dominate the reported score:

`CER_l = (1/N_l) · Σ_{i: len_i=l} edit_distance(pred_i, target_i) / l`,  averaged as  `CER_score = (1/L) · Σ_{l=1}^{L} CER_l`

for spans of length `l = 1..L` (Assael et al. 2025, refining Assael et al. 2022's own version of the same metric). This stratification is built around a protocol of masking spans of a chosen, known character length; our masking is standard BERT-style random-15%-of-tokens MLM, with no designed span-length control, so length-stratification has no natural masked-span-length axis to stratify over here. We instead report one pooled ratio over every masked position in the evaluation split:

`CER = ( Σ_i edit_distance(pred_i, target_i) ) / ( Σ_i len(target_i) )`

where `pred_i`/`target_i` are the top-1 predicted and true WordPiece token's surface string at masked position `i` (continuation-piece `##` prefix stripped before comparison). This is a genuinely different, less strict protocol than Aeneas/Ithaca's own — a direct numeric comparison to their reported CER values would not be meaningful — but it gives a character-level number alongside the token-level metrics above, which is useful precisely because a WordPiece token is not a fixed unit a domain reader can picture the way a raw character count can.

## 5. Results

**Metrics.** We report mean reciprocal rank, top-1/top-3/top-5 accuracy, and a pooled character error rate (CER, defined in Section 4) for masked-token restoration — the first three matching Lazar et al. (2021)'s own metric choice for direct comparability — and accuracy alongside macro-averaged F1 for each of the four classification heads. Accuracy alone can mask collapsed performance on minority classes under the severe class imbalance described in Section 3, which our untrained baseline illustrates directly (non-trivial accuracy from majority-class prediction alone, macro-F1 near zero).

**Headline comparison.** On the held-out test split, the provenience-conditioned model reaches macro-F1 0.541 against a text-only 0.449 (+0.092). Table 1 gives the full untrained / text-only / vision(provenience) comparison across every metric above, including CER.

**The provenience-vision effect.** This effect was first observed, and shown not to be a single-run artifact, at an earlier, smaller label space of 12 provenience classes (later expanded to the current 36, see below): on the validation split (used during development, not the numbers reported above), the same provenience-conditioned configuration was trained twice independently and reached macro-F1 0.766 and 0.764 — 0.002 apart — against a validation-split text-only baseline of 0.725. Both independent runs cleared the noise floor established using the language head, which never receives the image in any run, yet whose own macro-F1 varied by as much as 0.05 between otherwise-identical runs from random initialization alone. Repeating the same conditioning experiment for period and genre, individually and jointly, showed no corresponding effect for either.

Following a full corpus rebuild this project undertook to fix a train/test tablet-id leak and a Proto-Elamite/Linear-Elamite text-contamination issue — and, in the same pass, to expand provenience from 12 to the current 36 classes — both checkpoints were retrained once each on the rebuilt corpus and re-evaluated on the (also rebuilt) test split. The effect not only replicates but strengthens at this harder, higher-class-count label space: provenience macro-F1 rises from 0.449 (text-only) to 0.541 (vision), a gain of +0.092, against the noise-floor language head's own +0.023 move over the identical comparison (period and genre move by less than ±0.006, and restoration CER is unchanged at 0.312 vs 0.314 — as expected, since the image reaches only the provenience head). This later comparison is a single run per configuration rather than the dual-run check available at the original 12-class scale, but the margin between provenience's movement and every other head's is, if anything, wider here than at the original scale. Taken together, this is, to our knowledge, an independent confirmation — now at two different label-space scales, on a corpus of a different language and script and about a third the size of Aeneas's (56,934 documents against their 176,861), with a different vision backbone — of Aeneas's own finding that vision conditioning benefits geographic attribution specifically, and not other attribution tasks (Assael et al. 2025).

**Per-class breakdown.** The provenience gain is not distributed evenly across classes: it concentrates in classes with the fewest training examples (Šuruppak, F1 +0.403 on 10 test examples; Nimrud, +0.271 on 30; Adab, +0.267 on 28). For contrast, three of the language head's four classes move by no more than ±0.004 in F1; the exception, Bilingual, moves by +0.087, but it is also the smallest class by far (22 examples), consistent with sampling noise rather than a systematic effect — the same pattern the original 12-class comparison showed, just with a different small class landing on the noisy side. **[Figure 2 — see spec below. ## 6. Conclusion

We present Nabu, to our knowledge the first system to combine Ithaca/Aeneas-style multi-task attribution with masked-token restoration and a vision-conditioned head for Akkadian. Where Lazar et al. (2021) established restoration alone for this language, and Aeneas established vision-conditioned attribution for Latin at a substantially larger data scale, we combine both directions and add two attribution tasks — genre and language — with no direct analogue in prior epigraphic systems. Our central empirical finding — that a tablet's photograph benefits provenience attribution specifically, and not period or genre attribution — independently reproduces Aeneas's own finding for a structurally different language, script, and corpus scale, evidence that the effect reflects a genuine property of the underlying data (tablet material, shape, and photographic convention correlating with findspot) rather than an artifact of one project's pipeline. We also report, without omission, that an alternative from-scratch character-level architecture was built and evaluated for this project and found unproductive relative to fine-tuned mBERT at comparable training cost.
